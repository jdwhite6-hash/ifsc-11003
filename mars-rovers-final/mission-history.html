Mission History

From First Wheel Tracks to Sample Collection — A Timeline of Mars Rover Missions

Spirit & Opportunity

Mission: Mars Exploration Rovers (MER-A and MER-B)

Launch Dates: June 10, 2003 (Spirit) | July 7, 2003 (Opportunity)

Landing Dates: January 3, 2004 (Spirit, Gusev Crater) | January 24, 2004 (Opportunity, Meridiani Planum)

Planned Mission Duration: 90 Martian days (~92 Earth days)

Actual Duration: 2,208 days — Spirit | 5,498 days — Opportunity

Mission Goals

Investigate Martian rocks and soils for evidence of past water activity, characterize the climate and geology of landing sites, and assess conditions relevant to the origin of life.

Launch & Landing Details

Both rovers were encased in airbag-cushioned landers and delivered via direct entry into the Martian atmosphere. Spirit landed in Gusev Crater, a large basin believed to have once held a lake. Opportunity touched down in Meridiani Planum, a flat plain rich in the mineral hematite — a marker often associated with water.

Major Discoveries

  • Opportunity discovered clear evidence of ancient liquid water at its landing site, including layered sedimentary rock and the mineral jarosite, which forms only in acidic water.
  • Spirit uncovered silica-rich soil near its Home Plate plateau — a strong indicator of past hydrothermal activity or water interaction.
  • Together, the rovers confirmed that Mars was once a wetter, potentially habitable world.

Accomplishments

  • Opportunity drove 28.06 miles (45.16 km) — the longest drive ever completed on another world.
  • Spirit survived being stuck in soft soil for over a year before finally losing contact in 2010.
  • NASA declared Opportunity's mission complete on February 13, 2019, after the rover failed to respond following a global dust storm that blocked its solar panels.

Key Technical Features

  • Solar-powered with rechargeable batteries
  • Panoramic cameras (Pancam) for high-resolution color imaging
  • Rock Abrasion Tool (RAT) to grind into rock surfaces
  • Miniature Thermal Emission Spectrometer (Mini-TES) for mineral analysis
  • Alpha Particle X-Ray Spectrometer (APXS) for elemental composition

Curiosity

Mission: Mars Science Laboratory (MSL)

Launch Date: November 26, 2011

Landing Date: August 6, 2012

Landing Site: Gale Crater, near Aeolis Mons (Mount Sharp)

Mission Status: Active

Mission Goals

Determine whether Mars ever offered environmental conditions favorable for microbial life, study the climate and geology of Gale Crater, and assess planetary habitability.

Launch & Landing Details

Curiosity was too large for airbag landing. Instead, NASA engineers devised the "Sky Crane" — a rocket-powered descent stage that lowered the rover on cables directly onto the Martian surface. The maneuver worked flawlessly and has since been called one of the most audacious engineering achievements in space exploration history.

Major Discoveries

  • Confirmed that Gale Crater once held a lake with fresh, drinkable water — conditions that could have supported microbial life billions of years ago.
  • Detected organic molecules preserved in ancient mudstone, the building blocks of life as we know it.
  • Identified seasonal fluctuations in methane levels in the Martian atmosphere — a finding still under investigation as a possible sign of biological or geological activity.
  • Measured radiation levels on the Martian surface, crucial data for planning future human missions.

Accomplishments

  • Over 4,300 Martian days (sols) of operation and counting.
  • Has driven more than 19 miles (31 km) across Gale Crater.
  • Drilled into dozens of rock targets, providing the most detailed geochemical analysis of any planetary surface beyond Earth.

Key Technical Features

  • Nuclear-powered via Radioisotope Thermoelectric Generator (RTG) — no dependence on sunlight
  • ChemCam laser that vaporizes rock at a distance for spectral analysis
  • Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument suite: the most complex analytical lab ever sent to another planet
  • MARDI descent imager captured a full-color video of the landing
  • 17 cameras total

Perseverance

Mission: Mars 2020

Launch Date: July 30, 2020

Landing Date: February 18, 2021

Landing Site: Jezero Crater (ancient river delta)

Mission Status: Active

Mission Goals

Search for signs of ancient microbial life (biosignatures), collect and cache rock and soil samples for future return to Earth, test oxygen production on Mars, and demonstrate helicopter flight.

Launch & Landing Details

Like Curiosity, Perseverance used the Sky Crane system, upgraded with Terrain-Relative Navigation — a computer vision system that allowed the rover to identify and avoid hazards during descent in real time. It landed precisely within Jezero Crater, selected because its ancient river delta is one of the most promising places on Mars to find preserved biosignatures.

Major Discoveries

  • Confirmed the presence of an ancient delta environment in Jezero Crater, where sediment and organic material could have been deposited and preserved.
  • Collected over 20 rock core samples, stored in sealed titanium tubes on the surface for eventual retrieval.
  • MOXIE (Mars Oxygen In-Situ Resource Utilization Experiment) successfully produced oxygen from the Martian atmosphere — a first, and a critical technology for future human missions.
  • Ingenuity helicopter completed 72 flights before ending its mission in January 2024, vastly exceeding its 5-flight design plan.

Key Technical Features

  • Sky Crane landing with Terrain-Relative Navigation
  • SHERLOC (Scanning Habitable Environments with Raman & Luminescence for Organics & Chemicals) — ultraviolet Raman spectrometer for detecting organic compounds
  • PIXL (Planetary Instrument for X-ray Lithochemistry) — fine-scale elemental analysis
  • Two microphones — first to record audio on Mars
  • Mastcam-Z: zoomable stereo camera system
  • Carried the Ingenuity Mars Helicopter
